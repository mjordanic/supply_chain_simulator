# Introduction

A replenishment buyer decides, every day, how much of each product to order, from which supplier, before knowing the demand that will arrive, the fraction of the order the supplier will ship, or the cash and shelf space the other products in the same shop will consume. The decision is repeated for a year. Inventory bought today is paid for today and arrives after a lead time. Demand that finds an empty shelf is lost. Holding cost, order fees, and a shared cash account make an order that looks locally sensible expensive once the rest of the assortment is taken into account.

The usual answer is a textbook reorder rule. An order-up-to policy, a reorder-point policy, and their periodic-review cousins estimate a demand rate from recent sales and place an order when the inventory position falls short of a coverage target. The rate is the mean of realised sales. Realised sales are censored: on a day with no stock the recorded sale is zero even if customers would have bought. The rule therefore treats a stockout as evidence that demand was low, and it has no model of how a supplier's fill rate, a second supplier's longer lead time, or a shared capacity pool will change the value of the next order. Those effects are left to the parameters. In this study the parameters of one order-up-to rule are chosen by a black-box search on the same simulator the rule is later scored in. That tuned rule is the anchor.

A joint-embedding predictive architecture (JEPA) offers a different split of the same problem. The buyer does not learn a reward. It learns a model of what the shop's state will look like after an order is filled, and a separate planner reads that model at decision time. The model is trained to match a latent code of the future observation, not to reconstruct the observation in its original units. A small probe, trained with the encoder held fixed, translates the code back into on-hand inventory, in-transit units, and sales when the planner needs those quantities to add up a profit. The planner proposes a few coverage levels per product, imagines the profit of each proposal over a horizon longer than one lead time, and emits the proposal with the highest imagined profit. Because the cost of holding inventory enters only at planning time, the same frozen weights can be asked what they would order if holding became more expensive. No new trajectories are collected and the network is not fit again.

That split is the claim this study was built to test. A learned world model plus a planner should beat a tuned textbook policy on a replenishment task where the textbook family leaves money on the table, and the same weights should transfer to a different holding cost, to products that were held out of the training log, and to a disruption regime the log never samples. The implementation is deliberately narrow. One shop orders. Factories follow a static production rule. The buyer does not set prices or promotions. Each product has its own network. The search is a factored cross-entropy method over four coverage levels, not a learned policy gradient and not a full joint optimisation over the assortment. The code lives in `src/jepa/` and does not import the reinforcement-learning stack.

## What an earlier run could not show

A first published run of this design reported a learned buyer at 0.364 `net_profit / initial_cash` against a tuned order-up-to anchor at 3.031, and both of its pass rules failed. An audit recorded in the second-attempt specification (`.scratch/jepa-replenishment-v2/PRD.md`) found that those numbers were not a measurement of the architecture.

The planner scored an order over a window equal to the delivery lead time. The order was charged inside the window, and the units arrived at or after the window's edge, so the proposal that ordered least won even when the model of inventory was replaced by an exact recursion. On the same seeds that exact recursion scored 0.336 against the learned model's 0.333. The learned buyer placed orders on 7 of 365 ticks. A search that loses to uniform draws from its own action set has a broken objective.

The fit named `epochs` a count of full-batch gradient steps. The published command used 20. On a held-out episode split the checkpoint was no better than predicting the training mean, while ordinary least squares on the same inputs reached R² of about 0.94, 0.75, and 0.65 for next on-hand, next in-transit, and next sales. With an exponential-moving-average decay of 0.99, twenty updates left the target encoder close to its random initialisation, and that random projection was the regression target.

The transition log stored the quantity the buyer requested. Allocation then cut that quantity by supplier availability, cash, and capacity. The model was trained on an action that had not been taken.

The market added the seasonal cycle into the demand level, so the level integrated the sine wave and pinned against its clamps for most of the year. Factories were stocked far above demand and sold at unit cost, so fill rate did not vary and routing never mattered. Capacity and cash did not bind. On that world a 150-trial parameter search bought no reliable uplift, and a frozen planner placed the same orders at two holding rates. The profit drop between those rates was the world charging more for an unchanged order sequence.

The study reported here is the rebuild that followed that audit. The seasonal cycle is applied when demand is read (`Market.demand_multiplier`, ADR 0030). Supply is scarce and multi-sourced (`sample_episode`, ADR 0031). Shelf capacity and opening cash are sized from episode demand (`_pools_from_tightness`, ADR 0032). The log records the quantity Allocation filled (`_annotate_steps`). The planner scores four lead times and credits ending inventory (`ReplenishmentPlanner._score`). Training is a shuffled pass over the data, with a held-out episode split, early stopping, and an open-loop unroll as long as the planning horizon (`LatentDynamics.fit`). A profit table is written only when held-out rollout accuracy beats least squares (`publish_synthetic_comparison`). A holding-rate curve is written only when the frozen planner's order sequence is not the same at every rate (`assert_order_sequences_differ`).

## What this paper reports

The numbers below are the full published run: 896 simulated years in the training log, a latent model and a direct model fit at embedding width 256 and depth 4, five 150-trial order-up-to studies, and a paired evaluation on 32 seeds in each of three generalisation protocols plus a five-point holding-rate sweep. They are copied from `docs/jepa-replenishment.md`, which `src/jepa/paper.py` renders from the run artifacts. Raw trajectories stay under `runs/jepa-replenishment/` and are not part of the repository. Where the draft prints a refusal, a missing file, or a non-finite cell, this paper keeps that outcome.

The run does not support the claim. On the tuner holdout the tuned order-up-to anchor earns about 1.02 `net_profit / initial_cash`. The learned planner, scored on the same seeds inside the holding-rate sweep, earns about 0.38. Its profit series correlates with the never-order series at 0.9987, so the comparison writer omits it from the in-distribution table and the pass rule cannot be evaluated. The direct predictor, which is more accurate as a forecaster (mean rollout R² 0.742 against 0.432 for the latent model), is omitted for the same reason. The holding-rate sweep was written, so the frozen orders are not identical across rates, and at the three lowest rates the paired interval of latent minus the rate-specific anchor lies entirely below zero. An M5 replay exam is specified and was not scored: the run has no `m5/exam.parquet`.

The rest of the paper is organised so that the experiment can be rebuilt from the text. The methodology names every processing step and the function that implements it. The results section copies the published cells and says what each comparison is allowed to mean. The discussion treats the failure as a property of the design: a world that now has headroom, a forecaster that clears a weak baseline, and a planner whose orders still track buying nothing.
